⌨️ Typing Check-In Key

Everything needed to run a typing check-in: the TypingClub settings, the run steps, what earns credit, and the exact passage to paste.

What a check-in looks like

Every check-in runs the same way. Once students know it, the whole thing costs about three minutes of class.

  1. Everyone opens the test and takes their hands off the keyboard. Wait for every screen.
  2. Count them in — "three, two, one, go." Everyone starts on the same beat.
  3. Run the clock. No talking.
  4. Everyone writes down their two numbers: WPM and accuracy.
One standing rule: nobody announces their number out loud. Not at a check-in, not afterwards, not ever. Students are measured against their own previous best, and that only holds if the room isn't quietly ranking itself. Rotation 4 is where you teach this and the countdown; after that it should run itself.

Why Baseline (R1), Check-in 1 (R4) and Check-in 2 (R6) use a bare-minimum target (5 WPM / 50%). TypingClub won't let the target fields sit empty, so these three use a floor low enough that no student will feel anchored to it. A real target would draw a goal line on the student's screen — at the baseline and at Check-in 1 that would anchor students to a number before they have any business having one, and at Check-in 2 it would undercut the whole point of the Accuracy Gauntlet, which is that speed doesn't matter that day.

Setup — same every time

TypingClub teacher dashboard → Typing Tests tab → create a new test → set the duration → paste the passage → assign to your class. Make a fresh test each time; never re-assign an old one. Results give you adjusted WPM (speed minus errors) and accuracy per student.

Four rules
  • Use the passage listed for that rotation — never reuse one, or students memorize it.
  • Lowercase only, all quarter. No capitals, numbers, or symbols; students haven't been taught them yet.
  • Nobody finishes the passage. They're intentionally too long. Reaching a quarter of the way through is normal.
  • Check-ins run on even rotations only — R4, 6, 8, 10, 12, 14, 16, 18, 20. Odd rotations are normal typing practice.
R1 · Baseline Bank · 3 min · 0 pts, ungraded
Settings: 3 minutes · Target WPM: 5 · Target accuracy: 50%
How it runs
  • Say it plainly before you start: this is not a grade and not a race. It sets their own starting number for the year.
  • Walk the room while they type and fix posture and finger placement. Form starts on day one, not at the first graded test.
  • Every student records their WPM and accuracy somewhere permanent — notebook cover, pinned Canvas comment. Later check-ins compare against this number, so it can't be lost.

your memory is not a recording. every time you remember something, your brain rebuilds it from pieces, and it fills any gaps with what seems likely. this is why two people can walk away from the same afternoon holding two different versions of it, and both of them can be certain they are right. researchers have shown this in careful studies. they ask people to read a list of related words, then later ask which words were on the list. people confidently name words that were never there, as long as those words fit the pattern of the others. the brain is not being lazy. it is doing what it evolved to do, which is find the gist of a thing and hold onto that rather than store every detail. this has real weight in courtrooms, where a witness who feels sure is often believed more than a witness who hesitates. confidence and accuracy are not the same thing, and the gap between them has changed how careful investigators handle what people tell them. the useful lesson is smaller and more personal. if you want to hold onto something, write it down close to when it happened. do not trust that a clear memory is a correct one. your brain is a storyteller first and a filing cabinet second, and it would rather give you a good story than an exact one.

Check-in 1 · R4 · The Routine · 1 min · 5 pts, completion
Settings: 1 minute · Target WPM: 5 · Target accuracy: 50%
How it runs
  • Nobody is being measured today. This is the rotation where students learn the countdown so it's automatic for the rest of the year.
  • Run it exactly as described in "What a check-in looks like" above. Slow down and narrate it the first time.
  • Say this before you start, because it prevents a lot of discouragement: your number today may be lower than your baseline, and that is supposed to happen. They're learning correct fingers instead of the hunt-and-peck they walked in with, and that always slows people down before it speeds them up.
  • Do not record WPM or accuracy in the gradebook this rotation. Students record their own numbers; that's all.

Check-in 3 · R8 · Blind Draw · 1 min · 5 pts

R10 · GRADED — Midquarter · 3 min · 50 pts
Settings: 3 minutes · Target WPM: 20 · Target accuracy: 88%
How it runs
  • Remind them the form deduction is live: 10 points off for hunt-and-peck, wrong fingers, or looking down at the keyboard.
  • Say out loud that nobody finishes the passage and that this is expected.
  • Run 3 minutes. Circulate and note form problems on paper as you go — you cannot judge form from the results screen afterwards.
  • Score against 20 WPM / 88% using the sliding scale, then apply any form deductions.

Check-in 6 · R14 · Beat Your PR · 1 min · 5 pts
Settings: 1 minute · Target WPM: 22 · Target accuracy: 88%
How it runs
  • Students look up their baseline number from the Baseline (R1) and write it at the top of a scrap of paper before starting.
  • Run the minute using passage 1. One attempt.
  • Compare to their baseline, never to the class.
  • This lands ten sessions in, right after the Heatmap check-in, so most students will clear their baseline comfortably. That timing is deliberate — asking for it any earlier sets them up to fail while their form is still being rebuilt.
  • Passages 2 and 3 are spares: make-up tests, or a second attempt if you have time.

Check-in 7 · R16 · Speed vs. Accuracy Lab · 2 × 1 min · 5 pts
Settings: 1 minute, run twice · Target WPM: 23 · Target accuracy: 90%
How it runs
  • Run 1: tell them to type as fast as they possibly can and ignore mistakes. Record adjusted WPM and accuracy.
  • Run 2: tell them to type perfectly clean and go as slowly as they need. Record both numbers again.
  • Students submit all four numbers plus one answer: which run had the higher adjusted WPM?
  • Don't tell them the answer in advance. Most discover the clean run scored higher, and finding that out themselves is the entire lesson.
Full credit: both runs completed, all four numbers submitted, and the question answered. Goal: 23 WPM / 90%.

the layout of the keyboard you are using was not designed to help you type. it was designed around the limits of a machine that no longer exists, and it has outlasted that machine by well over a century. early typewriters had a mechanical problem. each key swung a metal arm, and if you struck two neighboring arms in quick succession they collided and jammed. the fix was to scatter the most common letter pairs to opposite sides of the keyboard, so the arms would have time to fall back before their neighbor came up. that scattering is why the letters sit in the strange order they do. it was a solution to a problem of springs and levers, and it worked. then the machine changed and the layout did not. typists had already learned it, schools were already teaching it, and every new machine had to match what people already knew or nobody would buy it. alternative layouts have been designed since, some of them measurably faster, and none of them ever caught on. the cost of retraining every typist in the world was always higher than the gain. this pattern shows up everywhere once you look for it. the width of railway tracks, the position of pedals in a car, the way dates are written in different countries. these are not the best available answers. they are the first answers that worked well enough, locked in place by everyone else already using them. economists call it path dependence, and it explains a surprising amount of why the world looks the way it does. there is something freeing in knowing this. the arrangement under your fingers is not a law of nature and it is not optimal. it is a hundred year old workaround for a jam in a machine you will never see. you are still going to learn it, because everyone else did, and that is exactly the point.

Why the early passages read strangely

Through Rotation 6 students only know the home row and the top row — no z, x, c, v, b, n or m, and no comma or period, because those live on the bottom row. That rules out the words and, in, on, not, can and be, which is why Check-in 1 (R4) and Check-in 2 (R6) are word lists rather than sentences. Check-in 3 (R8) gains commas and three more letters, so it reads as comma-spliced phrases. From the Midquarter test (R10) on the full alphabet is available and the passages are normal prose. Tell students why — they find it interesting rather than annoying.
